## 0. What this document actually is

An ICF is three legally distinct documents fused into one stapled packet, and the fusion is the source of most of the confusion participants experience:

1. **A consent disclosure** governed by 21 CFR 50 (FDA) and, where applicable, 45 CFR 46 (the Common Rule). This is the part that must be *understandable*.
2. **A HIPAA authorization** governed by 45 CFR 164.508. This is a permission slip for data flow and is legally unrelated to consent. It has its own required elements and its own signature.
3. **A set of optional research authorizations** — genomic analysis, biospecimen banking, future unspecified use — each of which must be separately declinable without affecting the main study.

A participant signs once, twice, or four times depending on what they agree to. Sites routinely collapse these into a single signature line, which is the single most common consent finding in FDA BIMO inspections after "procedures performed before consent."

The document also carries a fourth, unstated function: it is the **evidentiary record** that a conversation took place. The form is not the consent. The conversation is the consent; the form documents it. Every design decision below follows from that.

| (a)(4) | Disclosure of **appropriate alternative procedures or courses of treatment** | §10 What other choices do I have? |
| (a)(5) | Statement describing the **extent of confidentiality** of records, **and** noting the possibility that **FDA may inspect the records** | §14 Confidentiality and who will see my records |

| (a)(7) | Explanation of **whom to contact** for answers about the research, about **subjects' rights**, and in the event of a **research-related injury** — these must be distinguishable, and the rights contact must not be the investigator | §17 Whom do I call? |

**Design notes.**

- (a)(1) is the element sites under-serve. "Description of the procedures to be followed" is not satisfied by a schedule-of-activities table lifted from the protocol. It requires a participant to be able to picture their Tuesday. The drafted ICF therefore gives a narrative walkthrough *and* a table, with duration and blood volume per visit.
- (a)(5) contains a trap: the confidentiality statement must be *accurate*, not reassuring. An ICF that promises "your records will be kept strictly confidential" and then lists nine parties who will read them is internally inconsistent. The drafted text states the limit first ("we cannot promise complete privacy") and then enumerates.
- (a)(7) requires **three** functionally distinct contacts. A single "call the study team" line fails the element. The IRB contact exists specifically so a participant with a complaint about the investigator has somewhere to go that is not the investigator.

## 3. The Revised Common Rule key-information requirement — 45 CFR 46.116(a)(5)(i)

### 3.1 The requirement

> "Informed consent must begin with a concise and focused presentation of the key information that is most likely to assist a prospective subject … in understanding the reasons why one might or might not want to participate. This part of the informed consent must be organized and presented in a way that facilitates comprehension."

Paired with 46.116(a)(4): the consent as a whole must be organized to facilitate comprehension, and 46.116(a)(5)(ii): the form may not be merely a list of isolated facts.

### 3.2 Why it exists

Because the pre-2018 consent form failed empirically. Consent forms grew from ~4 pages to ~20 as sponsors and institutions added defensive language; comprehension studies found participants routinely could not state the study purpose, could not state that they might receive placebo, and believed the study was designed for their personal benefit — **therapeutic misconception**, the single most-documented failure in the consent literature. The 2018 rule's response was structural rather than substantive: it did not remove any disclosure, it mandated that the decision-relevant subset be pulled to the front and presented as an argument for and against joining, not as a summary.

The five things SACHRP identifies as usually belonging in key information:
1. That this is **research**, participation is **voluntary**, and the purpose is to produce generalizable knowledge — not to treat you.
2. A **summary of the study** — what will happen, in what order, for how long.
3. The **reasonably foreseeable risks** that would matter most to a reasonable person deciding.
4. The **benefits** that may reasonably be expected — including, honestly, none.
5. The **alternatives**, including the alternative of not participating and continuing standard care.

For this trial, the key information must lead with the fact that carries the most decisional weight and that participants most often miss: **a 1-in-4 chance of receiving placebo for six months while having moderate-to-severe eczema.** Anything that buries that fails the rule regardless of page count.

### 3.3 Applicability nuance — worth stating precisely

This study is industry-sponsored, FDA-regulated, and not federally conducted or supported. Strictly, the **Common Rule does not apply**; 21 CFR 50 does, and 21 CFR 50.25 has no key-information element. FDA proposed harmonizing 21 CFR 50/56 with the 2018 Common Rule but the FDA regulation as written still lacks the requirement.

In practice this is a distinction without a difference:
- Central IRBs (here, **Keystone IRB**) impose the key-information format as a condition of approval across their whole portfolio rather than maintaining two templates.
- Sponsor master-ICF templates are written to the stricter standard so a single template works for federally supported sites, VA sites, and academic sites operating under an institution-wide Common Rule commitment (FWA "check-the-box" legacy policies).
- **ICH E6(R3)** independently requires that information be presented so participants can understand it, which converges on the same structure.

So: the drafted ICF opens with KEY INFORMATION, and the outline records that it does so by **IRB requirement and sponsor template convention**, not by force of 21 CFR 50.

## 4. ICH E6(R3) consent requirements

E6(R3) (Good Clinical Practice, Principles + Annex 1; ICH Step 4, January 2025) restructures consent around **participant comprehension and the consent process**, not the artefact. Points that shape the drafted ICF:

- **Consent is a process, not an event.** Information must be provided in a way and at a pace that lets the participant consider it, ask questions, and consult others. The ICF's signature block therefore captures the **date *and time*** of signature — the audit trail for "was there time between the discussion and the first procedure?"
- **Plain language and proportionate length.** E6(R3) explicitly cautions against unnecessary complexity and against information that does not assist the decision. This is the first GCP text to treat over-disclosure as a defect rather than as caution.
- **Media flexibility.** Electronic consent, video, and layered presentation are permitted, provided the participant can retain a copy and the process is documented. Site 1047 uses paper; the ICF is drafted so a layered/eConsent build would fall out of it without restructuring.
- **Ongoing consent.** New safety information that could affect willingness to continue must be communicated; the ICF must state this (which also satisfies 21 CFR 50.25(b)(5)).
- **Non-coercive setting.** The person obtaining consent must be qualified and delegated. At Site 1047 the delegation log assigns the consent discussion to **Priya Raghunathan, BSN, RN, CCRC** with **PI countersignature**; that arrangement dictates the two-line "person conducting the consent discussion" plus investigator attestation structure.
- **Vulnerability and undue influence.** Payment must be disclosed and must not be coercive. $125/visit × 12 visits is disclosed in full, with the tax consequence, so the participant can evaluate it.
- **Documented copy to the participant.** E6(R3) and 21 CFR 50.27(a) both require it; the ICF ends with an explicit acknowledgement line.

### 4.1 EU CTR 536/2014 — what differs

The trial is also an EU trial (CT number 2022-501538-44), so the master ICF must be adaptable to Regulation (EU) No 536/2014. Differences from the US instrument that matter to the template:

Sites that copy from the master ICF's EU annexes create documents that promise things the US sponsor has not agreed to. The drafted ICF is US-only and says so nowhere — it simply contains nothing EU-specific.

## 5. HIPAA authorization — 45 CFR 164.508

### 5.1 Why it is separate

HIPAA is not a human-subjects protection. It is a **use-and-disclosure rule binding on covered entities** — here, Cascade Dermatology as a treating provider that holds protected health information (PHI). The sponsor is *not* a covered entity. The legal question HIPAA answers ("may this clinic release your medical record to Meridian and its CRO?") is different from the question consent answers ("do you agree to be injected with an experimental antibody?").

Consequences that the document design must reflect:

- **A person may consent to the study and refuse the HIPAA authorization — but then cannot enroll**, because the study cannot be conducted without disclosing PHI. 45 CFR 164.508(b)(4)(i) expressly permits conditioning **research-related treatment** on the authorization. This is the *only* place where conditioning is permitted, and the form must say so accurately: your ordinary clinical care at this clinic is not conditioned on it; your participation in this study is.
- **Revocation has a different shape from withdrawal.** Revoking the authorization stops *future* uses and disclosures but does not reach anything already used or disclosed in reliance on it, and the sponsor may continue to use data already collected to preserve the integrity of the research (164.508(b)(5)(i) reliance exception). This is the point participants most often misunderstand: "I quit and they still have my data" is legally correct and must be stated before signature, not discovered after.
- **Revocation must be in writing.** The form must give the address to write to.
- **Redisclosure warning.** Once PHI goes to Meridian, Harborlight Clinical Research, or Meridian Central Laboratories, it is out of HIPAA's reach and may be redisclosed. This must be stated.
- Combined ("compound") authorizations: 164.508(b)(3)(i) permits combining the research authorization with the consent document, and 164.508(b)(3)(iii) permits combining a conditioned and an unconditioned research authorization (i.e. main study + optional banking) **only if** the form clearly differentiates them and allows the individual to opt in to the unconditioned one separately. That rule is the direct reason the optional sub-studies get their own initials boxes.

## 7. Optional and separate consents

### 7.1 Optional genomic (DNA) sample — Day 1, one draw

Per the canon SoA, the optional genomic sample is drawn **once, at Day 1**, and requires **separate consent**. Drafting requirements:

- Its own explanation of what DNA is and what will be looked at (genes related to atopic dermatitis, immune response, and how people respond to rocatinlimab).
- Explicit statement of what will **not** be done: no whole-genome sequencing reported back, no clinical diagnosis, **no results returned** to the participant or the doctor. Under 45 CFR 46.116(c)(7) (Common Rule additional element) a statement about whole genome sequencing is required when applicable; the drafted ICF states plainly that results are not returned and are not part of the medical record, which is the participant-relevant version of that element.
- **How long samples are kept and where** — a definite term (drafted: up to 20 years, then destroyed), and the coding scheme (coded, not anonymous; the site holds the key; the sponsor does not).
- The right to **withdraw the sample later** and what that means (destroyed if identifiable and not yet analysed; data already generated is retained).
- Its own **initials + signature**.
- The statement that declining **does not affect** main-study participation, medical care, or payment — repeated in the sub-study section rather than only in the key information, because that is where the decision is being made.

### 7.2 Optional storage of leftover samples for future AD research

Broader than the genomic sample: leftover serum/plasma from PK, ADA, and biomarker draws. Requirements mirror 7.1, plus:

- **Future unspecified use.** 45 CFR 46.116(b)(9) requires either a statement that identifiers may be removed and the material used for future research without additional consent, **or** a statement that it will not be. The drafted ICF picks a lane and says which: leftover samples may be used for future research about atopic dermatitis and related immune diseases, and will not be sold.
- **Commercial value** — 45 CFR 46.116(c)(7)/(8) style disclosure: research using the samples may lead to products of commercial value; the participant will not share in any profit. Stating this is the honest thing and its absence is a recurring IRB deficiency.
- Scope limit: "research about atopic dermatitis and related immune conditions" is narrower than a true broad-consent biobank and is the right scope for a study-embedded optional sub-study. A genuine broad consent under 46.116(d) is a different instrument with its own required elements and is **not** what this is; do not use the phrase "broad consent" in the participant text.

### 7.3 GINA notice

The **Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008** must be described where genetic information is collected. The participant-facing statement must include both what GINA does and, critically, what it does **not** do:

- **Protects:** health insurers may not use genetic information to deny coverage or set premiums; employers may not use it in hiring, firing, or promotion decisions.
- **Does not protect:** **life insurance, disability insurance, and long-term care insurance.** Also does not apply to employers with fewer than 15 employees, and has limited application to the U.S. military, the Veterans Health Administration, and the Indian Health Service.

Omitting the "does not protect" half is a common and serious drafting failure — it converts a legal notice into false reassurance.

## 8. Readability, translation, LAR, witness, re-consent

### 8.1 Readability

- Target: **8th grade** (roughly Flesch-Kincaid 6–8). Most central IRBs, Keystone included, set 8th grade as a review criterion.
- Techniques used in the draft: second person throughout; short declarative sentences; every technical term defined at first use in parentheses ("an antibody (a protein made in a lab that sticks to one target)"); numbers expressed as natural frequencies ("about 1 in 10 people") alongside percentages; tables for anything with more than three parallel items; no nominalisations ("we will check" not "monitoring will be performed").
- What readability metrics do **not** capture, and what the game is about: a form can score 7th grade and still be incomprehensible to a person who is frightened, in pain, in a hurry, or who has been handed 22 pages in a waiting room. The formatting decisions — one page of key information, a visit table with times, an explicit blood-volume total in tablespoons — exist to serve comprehension where the score does not.

### 8.2 Language and translation

- 21 CFR 50.20 requires the information be in a language **understandable to the subject**; 50.25 requires the elements; 50.27 requires a written form the subject signs.
- For a participant who does not read English: either a **fully translated, IRB-approved long form** in that language, or the **short-form process** of 21 CFR 50.27(b)(2) — an IRB-approved short form in the participant's language, an oral presentation of the full English form, a **witness to the oral presentation** fluent in both languages, participant signs the short form, witness signs both, person obtaining consent signs the English form. Site 1047 uses the short-form pathway for unanticipated languages; DayLog ePRO supports 14 languages, but the eDiary is not a consent instrument and its language coverage does not substitute.
- The site-specific ICF should note the availability of an interpreter. The drafted document states it in §17.

### 8.3 Legally authorized representative (LAR)

- 21 CFR 50.3(l): an LAR is defined by **applicable law** — for adults in Oregon, that is generally a health-care representative under an advance directive, a court-appointed guardian, or a surrogate under ORS 127.635 hierarchy. The ICF does not define it; it provides a line and requires the relationship and the authority to be written in.
- The eligibility criteria for this trial (adult, capable of daily eDiary entry) make LAR consent unlikely, but the master ICF template carries the line and the site-specific version keeps it. A line that is unused and struck through with a "N/A" is correct; a line that is missing when needed is a protocol deviation.

### 8.4 Impartial witness

Two distinct uses, frequently conflated:
1. **Illiterate / cannot read participant** (ICH E6 §4.8.9): an impartial witness must be present for the **entire** consent discussion, and signs to attest that the information was accurately explained and that consent was freely given.
2. **Short-form translation** (21 CFR 50.27(b)(2)): the witness attests to the oral presentation, as above.

"Impartial" means independent of the trial and not unfairly influenced by site staff. A study coordinator cannot be the impartial witness. A family member usually can, though some IRBs discourage it. The ICF must carry the line **and** the attestation wording, or the witness's signature means nothing.

### 8.5 Re-consent triggers

The site must re-consent when:
- A **new ICF version** is IRB-approved and the change is relevant to ongoing participants (new risk, changed procedure, changed duration, changed payment). Here: v4.0.1 approved 19-DEC-2023 — every participant still on study at that date must be re-consented at their next visit, and the re-consent must be documented in the source with a note on why.
- **New safety information** arises that could affect willingness to continue (21 CFR 50.25(b)(5); ICH E6(R3)).
- The participant's **status changes** — regains capacity, reaches age of majority, becomes pregnant (moves into a different information set).
- A **protocol amendment** adds procedures.
- The participant is **re-screened** after a screen failure (canon permits one re-screen) — a fresh consent is required for the new screening number.

Documentation of re-consent must record: the version consented to, the date and time, who conducted it, and that a copy of the new version was given. Continuing on an old version because "nothing changed for them" is a judgment the IRB makes, not the site.

## 9. What sites actually get wrong

Ordered roughly by frequency in FDA BIMO Form 483 observations and sponsor audit findings. These are the failure modes the game should be able to dramatise.

| Failure | Why it happens | Regulatory consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Any study procedure performed before the consent form is signed** — a screening lab, an ECG, even a research-only vital sign | The participant is already in the chair; the coordinator "will get the signature after"; the visit is running late | 21 CFR 50.20 violation. Data from the procedure are unusable. Repeat findings support disqualification. The single most cited consent observation. |
| **Consenting on a superseded version** | The IRB approves v4.0.1 on 19-DEC-2023; the copies in the consent room are v3.2; nobody purges the binder | Consent obtained is invalid as to the new information; requires re-consent and a deviation report. Version control is a site process failure, not a paperwork one. |
| **Missing date, or a date written by staff** | Participant signs and forgets to date; coordinator "helps" | The participant must date their own signature. A staff-written date is falsification of a source record, however well-intended. Correct fix: participant dates it, or a note-to-file with the participant's own later attestation. |
| **The coordinator signs before the participant** | The coordinator pre-signs a stack of forms to save time; or signs at the top of the page as they hand it over | Makes it facially impossible to demonstrate the discussion preceded the agreement. The consenter's signature must be dated **on or after** the participant's, never before. |
| **Signature date/time inconsistent with visit source** | Consent timed 09:15 but the screening blood draw is timed 09:05 | Proves procedures preceded consent. This is why the ICF captures **time**, not just date, and why the game's inspection mechanic can hinge on two timestamps. |
| **No documented copy given to the participant** | Copier jam; "I'll mail it" | 21 CFR 50.27(a). Requires an explicit acknowledgement line and a source note. |
| **Missing PI signature where site SOP requires countersignature** | The PI is in clinic; the form goes in the binder unsigned | SOP-001 at Site 1047 requires PI countersignature. Failure to follow your own SOP is a finding even when the regulation does not require the signature. |
| **Optional sub-study initials left blank** | Participant not asked, or asked but boxes not completed | Ambiguous authorization. Samples cannot be used; if they were, that is unauthorized research use. Blank ≠ "no" for audit purposes — it means the conversation is undocumented. |
| **HIPAA authorization signature missing** | Collapsed into the main signature | Unauthorized disclosure of PHI by the covered entity. Distinct from the consent finding and reportable separately. |
| **Illegible or absent printed names** | Only signatures collected | Cannot identify who obtained consent; cannot verify they were delegated. Print lines exist so the delegation log can be checked against them. |
| **Consent obtained by an undelegated person** | Front-desk staff, or a new coordinator not yet on the DOA log | 21 CFR 312.60 / delegation failure. Every consent obtained by that person is questionable. |
| **Alterations, whiteout, backdating** | Correcting an error | ALCOA+ violation. Correct method: single line-through, initial, date, brief reason. Never obliterate. |
| **Re-consent not performed after an amendment** | No tracking of who is still active vs. completed | Participants continue on information the IRB has superseded. |
| **Assuming the form is the consent** | Culture | The deepest one. A perfectly executed form documenting a 90-second conversation in a hallway satisfies the auditor and fails the participant. This is the gap the game is about. |

## 10. The signature block — structure and why every line exists

The block is not a formality; each line answers a specific question an inspector, an IRB, or a plaintiff's attorney will ask.

```
PARTICIPANT
  Printed name ______________________  ← who is this, in legible form
  Signature ________________________   ← the agreement itself
  Date ____________  Time ______ ☐AM ☐PM  ← participant-written; proves consent preceded procedures

LEGALLY AUTHORIZED REPRESENTATIVE (if applicable)
  Printed name ______________________
  Signature ________________________
  Relationship to participant _______  ← establishes standing
  Authority (describe) ______________  ← 45 CFR 164.508(c)(1)(vi) requires the basis of authority
  Date ____________  Time ______

IMPARTIAL WITNESS (required if the participant cannot read, or for short-form consent)
  Attestation wording, then:
  Printed name ______________________
  Signature ________________________   ← attests the information was accurately explained
  Date ____________  Time ______

PERSON CONDUCTING THE CONSENT DISCUSSION
  Printed name ______________________  ← checked against the delegation log
  Signature ________________________   ← attests the discussion happened and questions were answered
  Date ____________  Time ______       ← must be on or after the participant's

PRINCIPAL INVESTIGATOR / SUB-INVESTIGATOR (per SOP-001)
  Printed name ______________________
  Signature ________________________
  Date ____________                    ← investigator's acceptance of responsibility for the process

ACKNOWLEDGEMENT
  ☐ I received a signed and dated copy of this form.  ← 21 CFR 50.27(a)
  ☐ I received a copy of the HIPAA Authorization.     ← 45 CFR 164.508(c)(4)
```

Rationale summary:

| Line | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Printed name | Can we read who this is without handwriting analysis? |
| Signature | Did they agree? |
| Date | Did the agreement precede the research? |
| **Time** | Did the agreement precede the *first procedure that day*? Only a time can answer this when consent and screening happen in one visit. |
| LAR relationship + authority | Does this person have legal standing to decide? |
| Witness attestation | Was the information actually explained to someone who could not read it? |
| Consenter printed name | Was this person delegated to obtain consent? |
| Consenter date/time | Was it on or after the participant's? |
| Investigator signature | Does the PI accept responsibility, per site SOP? |
| Copy acknowledgement | 21 CFR 50.27(a) satisfied and evidenced. |
| Separate HIPAA signature | 45 CFR 164.508 satisfied independently of consent. |
| Separate sub-study initials | 45 CFR 164.508(b)(3)(iii) — conditioned and unconditioned authorizations differentiated. |

**Footer.** Every page carries `Protocol 20210143 · ICF v4.0.1 · 29-NOV-2023 · Site 1047 · Page __ of __`. Page-of-page numbering is what lets a monitor prove no page is missing from a filed copy; version and date on every page are what prevent a mixed-version packet — the physical form of the version-control failure in §9.

## 12. Drafting constraints carried from canon

- 12 visits exactly, named as in STUDY_FACTS §5. No visit invented, removed, or re-timed.
