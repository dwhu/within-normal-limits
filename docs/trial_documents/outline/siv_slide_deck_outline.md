## 1. What a Site Initiation Visit deck actually is

### 1.1 The two things happening in the room at once

An SIV deck is the only document in this corpus that is not primarily a *reference*. Nobody looks
things up in an SIV deck. It exists to be **performed** — read aloud, in a room, once — and then
filed.

Two things are happening simultaneously while it is performed, and the deck has to serve both:

1. **Knowledge transfer.** Eleven people who have never run this protocol are being told how to run
   it. Some of them (the PI, the lead coordinator) have run nine AD trials and want the deltas. Some
   of them (a pharmacist who is on site two days a week, a phlebotomist who will see the protocol
   only through the lens of a kit and a centrifuge) need the whole thing from first principles. The
   deck has to be simultaneously a briefing and an orientation, which is why real SIV decks are long
   and shallow rather than short and deep.
2. **Compliance evidence.** The deck, plus the signed attendance log stapled to it, is the sponsor's
   documentary proof that it discharged a legal obligation to train the site before a single
   participant was exposed to an investigational product. That evidence will be read, years later,
   by a person who was not in the room, who is hostile to the site's interests, and who will
   conclude from silence that the training did not happen.

The second purpose is why an SIV deck contains slides that everyone in the room already knows. A
slide titled "Serious Adverse Events — The Six Criteria" exists not because Priya Raghunathan, RN,
CCRC, eleven years in the field, does not know the six criteria. It exists so that on the day an
inspector asks how the site knew, there is a numbered slide, a dated deck, and her signature on the
log.

### 1.2 The regulatory function

**ICH E6(R3) §4.10 — Investigator qualifications and training.** The sponsor is required to ensure
that the investigator and site staff are qualified by education, training, and experience, and are
adequately informed about the protocol, the investigational product, and their trial-related duties.
E6(R3) frames this in terms of *quality by design* and *fitness for purpose*: training must be
proportionate to the risk the task carries, and it must be documented in a way that shows what was
covered, to whom, by whom, and when. E6(R3) also explicitly discourages training theatre — the
retraining-on-everything reflex — in favour of targeted, risk-proportionate training. The SIV deck is
the anchor event: the one occasion where "everything" is genuinely proportionate, because nobody has
been trained on anything yet.

The corresponding investigator-side obligations sit in E6(R3) §2 (the investigator agrees to conduct
the trial in compliance with the protocol; agrees to permit monitoring and inspection; is responsible
for supervising anyone to whom trial-related duties are delegated). The SIV is where those
obligations are read aloud in the presence of the people who will actually perform them.

**21 CFR 312.53 — Selecting investigators and monitors.** The sponsor shall select only investigators
qualified by training and experience, shall ship investigational drug only to investigators
participating in the investigation, and shall obtain from each investigator a signed Form FDA 1572,
a curriculum vitae, and a financial disclosure commitment (FDA 3454/3455 under 21 CFR Part 54).
§312.53(a) also requires the sponsor to ensure the investigator is *informed* of its obligations. In
practice, the SIV is where a sponsor demonstrates §312.53 compliance operationally: the deck covers
the protocol, the IB, and the site's obligations; the attendance log evidences who received it; and
the 1572 in the regulatory binder evidences that the investigator accepted them.

**21 CFR 312.57 and 312.62 — Recordkeeping.** The training record is an essential document under
E6(R3) Appendix C. It must be retained for the full retention period (25 years under EU CTR Art. 58,
per Protocol Amendment 3 §11.9; at least 2 years past marketing application decision under
312.57/312.62). It is the *signed attendance log*, not the deck, that is the essential document —
though in practice they are filed together, because a log that says "protocol training" without an
attached agenda proves almost nothing.

### 1.3 The failure mode that makes this document matter

The characteristic inspection finding is not "the site did the wrong thing." It is "the site did a
thing, and there is no record that anyone told them what the right thing was." A 483 observation
under this heading typically reads: *"Study personnel performed [protocol-specified procedure] prior
to documented training on the protocol"* or *"There is no documentation that [staff member], who
obtained informed consent from subjects 1047-003 and 1047-007, received training on the protocol or
the informed consent form."*

That second form is the one that catches sites. The attendance log is checked not against the roster
but against the **delegation log** and the **signed consent forms**. Anyone whose initials appear on
a source document, a consent form, or an eCRF audit trail must appear on a training record dated
*before* that entry. This is why the SIV attendance log needs a printed name column, a role column,
a signature column, and a date column filled in by hand, and why late arrivals cannot simply be added
to the list afterwards — they get a separate, honestly dated, individual training record.

## 2. How the SIV deck differs from every other document in this corpus

| Dimension | Protocol / SRM / manuals | SIV deck |
|---|---|---|
| Mode of use | Consulted, indexed, searched | Performed once, then filed |
| Lifespan of the content | Live until superseded | Frozen at the date it was presented |
| Authority | Normative — states requirements | Derivative — restates requirements, creates none |
| Primary regulatory function | Defines the trial | Evidences that the trial was explained |
| What proves it worked | Adherence in the data | A signature on the attendance log |
| Version control | Amendment-controlled, re-issued | Never re-issued; superseded by a *new training event* |
| Tone | Impersonal, precise | Spoken, hedged, occasionally blunt |

Four consequences follow, and the deck must be written with all four in mind.

**A. It is frozen in time.** The deck is dated 19-DEC-2022 and presented 21-DEC-2022. It cannot
know about Protocol Amendment 2 (12-MAY-2023) or Amendment 3 (29-NOV-2023). It therefore states
things that are, today, wrong — four AESI categories rather than five, no formal screening-extension
provision, a rescue-therapy rule that had not yet been written into the protocol body. This is not an
error in the document; it is what an archived training artefact *is*. A deck that had been quietly
updated to match the current protocol would be worse than useless: it would destroy its own evidentiary
value, because it would no longer show what was actually said in the room on 21-DEC-2022. The corpus
handles this by appending an **archival note** to the file copy, of the kind a regulatory coordinator
would add when indexing the ISF, pointing to the later retraining records.

**B. It is derivative and must say so.** The deck restates the protocol; it does not create
obligations. Where deck and protocol conflict, the protocol governs. An SIV deck that says something
the protocol does not say has manufactured a protocol deviation for every site that follows it.

**C. It is the last time the whole team is in one room.** After today the site team fragments by
function: Wen-Li Chao will see the study as a refrigerator and a kit log; Marisol Duarte will see it
as a draw order and a dry-ice shipment; Sam Oyelaran will see it as an essential-document index.
Nobody will convene all of them again until close-out, two years later. This makes the SIV the only
opportunity to install **shared mental models** rather than role-specific procedures: what the
co-primary endpoint is and why the rater's fifteen minutes matter more than anyone else's;
why a predose sample drawn after the injection cannot be fixed; why the phlebotomist's draw order
protects the immunogenicity dataset. Good SIV decks spend their speaker notes on the *seams* between
roles, because the seams are where sites fail.

**D. The speaker notes are the real document.** The slides carry the official line. The notes carry
what an experienced CRA actually says: which rules are commonly broken, which ones the sponsor will
detect, what the participants will actually do, and what will happen to the site if it takes a
shortcut. For the game, this gap — between the bullet and the aside — is the entire point. The site
is not being told what to do; it is being told what everyone does and why it is nevertheless not
allowed.

## 3. Standard agenda sections and why each exists

An SIV agenda is near-universal across sponsors. Sections are listed below with their function, the
minimum content required for the training record to hold up, and the pathology each guards against.

### 3.1 Title, welcome, purpose (slides 1–3)

Function: establishes the record — study, protocol number, version, date, site, presenters.
The title slide is the single most important slide for evidentiary purposes; it dates the training and
identifies the protocol version trained. A deck with no protocol version on the title slide cannot
prove *what* was trained.

### 3.2 Agenda and introductions (4–6)

Function: sets expectations for the day (a real SIV runs 4–6 hours) and, more importantly, puts the
site team on the record by name and role. The introductions slide is the human-readable precursor to
the delegation log, and the two should agree; if a name is on the SIV deck and not on the DOA log,
the CRA will ask why, and vice versa.

### 3.3 Background and rationale (7–12)

Function: motivation, not decoration. This section exists because a coordinator who understands *why*
the predose PK sample must precede the injection will protect it; one who does not will treat it as
paperwork. It also discharges an ethical obligation — the site team must be able to answer a
participant who asks "why is this drug different?" — and a practical one: an OX40-directed antibody
behaves unlike the IL-4Rα and JAK agents the site already knows, and the fever/chills signal will
otherwise surprise them.

Minimum content: disease burden, current treatment landscape and its gaps, mechanism, development
programme status, rationale for this specific study.

### 3.4 Study design (13–18)

Function: gives the site the shape of the trial in one picture. Must cover: phase, allocation ratio,
masking, duration, sample size and number of centres, objectives, and — critically — the **co-primary
endpoints**, because the co-primary determines which assessments are load-bearing. A site that knows
Week 24 rIGA 0/1 and EASI 75 are the co-primary will move heaven and earth to keep the Week 24 visit
in window. A site that does not will treat Week 24 like Week 8.

The 3:1 allocation deserves its own emphasis for a reason the deck should be honest about: it is a
recruitment tool. Participants will ask their odds. The answer is 75%, and saying it plainly is both
true and better for retention than hedging.

### 3.5 Schedule of Activities (19–24)

Function: the operational spine. Reproduced in full because the site will photograph these slides and
tape them inside a cupboard door. Must cover visit count, day numbers, windows, which visits are
dosing visits, and the order-of-operations rule at a dosing visit (efficacy and predose draws before
IP).

Pathology guarded against: window drift and post-dose "predose" samples — the two most common
avoidable deviations in a Q4W injectable trial.

### 3.6 Eligibility (25–32)

Function: the section that determines whether the trial's population is what the protocol says it is.
Must cover every inclusion and exclusion criterion — not a summary — because "training on the
protocol" that skipped exclusion criteria will not survive an inspection where an ineligible
participant was enrolled.

This section carries the deck's ethical centre of gravity: the **entry thresholds** (EASI ≥16,
vIGA-AD ≥3, BSA ≥10%, Worst Pruritus NRS ≥4). A borderline candidate creates a real, felt pressure:
the participant wants in, the site wants the enrolment, and the rater holds a score that is a
judgement call. Rounding EASI 15.4 to 16.2 is not a grey area; it is falsification of a research
record. The deck must say so out loud, and the speaker note must explain *how it is caught* — because
"it's wrong" persuades nobody who is already rationalising, while "the sponsor plots the distribution
of baseline EASI and a spike at 16.0–16.4 is visible from orbit" persuades everybody.

### 3.7 Informed consent (33–37)

Function: trains the **process**, not the form. The form is IRB-approved and self-explanatory; the
process is where findings come from. Must cover: version control and which version is current, who is
delegated to consent and who must countersign, the requirement that consent precede any
study-specific procedure, documentation standards, and re-consent triggers.

### 3.8 Investigational product (38–43)

Function: the section with the shortest distance between error and patient harm. Must cover
presentation, dose, regimen, storage and excursion handling, room-temperature equilibration,
administration technique and site rotation, accountability, and post-dose observation.

### 3.9 Efficacy assessments (44–52)

Function: protects the endpoints. Must cover rater certification, the same-rater rule, rater blinding
to prior scores, and the mechanics of each instrument. A worked EASI example is standard and worth the
slide: it converts an abstract instrument into a number the room can check.

### 3.10 The eDiary (53–56)

Function: the only dataset generated by the participant, unsupervised, 168 times over 24 weeks. Must
cover what it collects, the compliance threshold, how to train a participant, and — the section that
matters — **what the site may never do**, which is touch it. Every ePRO system audit-trails entry
timestamps; sites that "help" get caught.

### 3.11 Laboratory (57–60)

Function: the seam between the phlebotomist and everyone else. Must cover kits and requisitions, draw
order, predose PK/ADA timing, processing and shipping, and alert-value handling with the PI review
requirement.

### 3.12 Safety reporting (61–67)

Function: the section with statutory consequences. Must cover the AE definition and collection period,
the SAE seriousness criteria, the **24-hour reporting clock**, the rule that causality is assessed by
the investigator and nobody else, AESIs, pregnancy reporting, and the contact routes. The contact card
is a physical artefact handed out in the room, and the deck should show it.

### 3.13 Systems (68–72)

Function: IRT and EDC walkthrough, data-entry timelines, query handling, and emergency unblinding.
Emergency unblinding belongs here rather than in safety because it is a *system* action with a
safety trigger; sites that do not know where the button is will call the CRA, who is blinded.

### 3.14 Recruitment and retention (73–75)

Function: makes the enrolment commitment concrete and names the sources. Also the section where the
site's realistic constraints — competing AD trials, washout attrition, a 30-day screening window —
should be surfaced honestly rather than papered over.

### 3.15 GCP and site responsibilities (76–78)

Function: delegation, supervision, source documentation, deviation identification and reporting, and
inspection readiness. Short, but not skippable: it is the section that maps E6(R3) investigator
obligations onto named individuals in the room.

### 3.16 Activation requirements (79–80)

Function: the "green light" checklist. This is the most operationally consequential section of the
day, because it is the only one with a deadline. See §5.

### 3.17 Q&A and attendance log (final slides)

Function: the Q&A slide is not filler — questions asked at an SIV are logged, answered, and the
answers circulated, because an unanswered question at an SIV becomes a deviation in March. The
attendance log slide is the deck's regulatory payload.

**Absences.** Anyone not present must be trained separately, with an individually dated record, before
performing any delegated activity. The honest handling — a separate, later-dated record — is always
safer than adding a name to a log for a meeting the person did not attend, which is falsification of
a training record and, in an inspection, worse than the absence it conceals.

**Partial attendance.** A person who attends only part of the day should be recorded as such. In
practice sites rarely do this, and CRAs rarely insist; the deck should nonetheless make the standard
explicit so the game can render the pressure of a pharmacist who leaves at noon.

## 5. What activation ("green light") depends on

Site activation is a sponsor decision, communicated in writing, after every item below is verified.
Nothing may be screened before it. The checklist is the deck's final substantive content, and each
item has a named owner and a date.

**Regulatory and contractual**
1. Fully executed Clinical Trial Agreement (Site 1047: executed 12-DEC-2022).
2. IRB approval of the protocol, the site-specific ICF, and all recruitment materials, with the
   approval letters filed (Keystone IRB, initial approval 08-NOV-2022).
3. Reliance agreement in place where the site cedes review (Cascade: 04-NOV-2022).
4. Signed Form FDA 1572 with all sub-investigators listed; current CVs; current medical licences;
   FDA 3454/3455 financial disclosure for the PI and every sub-investigator.
5. Signed protocol signature page from the PI.
6. Delegation of Authority log signed by the PI, consistent with the training records.

**Training**
7. Protocol and ICF training documented for all staff (this SIV).
8. System training: IRT, EDC, ePRO — vendor-issued completion records.
9. Rater certification current for every person who will perform vIGA-AD/rIGA or EASI (DATG).
10. GCP training current for all research staff.
11. IATA dangerous-goods certification current for anyone shipping specimens.

**Operational readiness**
12. IP received, temperature-verified on receipt, and quarantined until release; refrigerator
    continuously monitored with alarm escalation tested.
13. Pharmacy/IP accountability logs and temperature logs in place.
14. Laboratory kits received, inventoried, and checked for expiry; central lab site set-up complete;
    courier account active.
15. eDiary devices received, charged, provisioned, and test-transmitted.
16. IRT site activated and user accounts live; EDC accounts provisioned and the site enabled.
17. Essential-document index complete and verified by the CRA against the sponsor's TMF.

**The final gate:** the sponsor issues a written **site activation / green-light notification**. The
date of first screening cannot precede it. At Site 1047 that notification is dated 06-JAN-2023,
sixteen days after the SIV — a normal interval, consumed almost entirely by drug shipment and IRT
activation.

## 6. Content the deck must NOT contain

- **Anything the protocol does not say.** No site-local rules, no "we prefer," no additional
  eligibility filters.
- **Unblinding information.** No treatment-arm identifiers, no kit-to-arm mapping, no PK ranges that
  would let a blinded rater infer assignment.
- **Future protocol content.** A deck dated 19-DEC-2022 that references Amendment 3 is a forgery.
- **Enrolment "guidance" that shades into pressure.** Sponsors do exert enrolment pressure; a deck
  that puts it in writing next to a slide on eligibility thresholds creates an inspection problem for
  the sponsor.
- **Participant-identifiable information**, including in worked examples. The EASI worked example uses
  a fictional composite.
- **Budget detail.** Payments are a contract matter between Amgen Clinical Contracts and Gregory
  Tarrant; a CRA discussing per-visit payments in a training session next to eligibility criteria is
  a governance error even when the numbers are correct.

## 8. Tone and register for the speaker notes

The notes are written as Kevin Ostrander and Rosalind Achebe would actually speak, with the following
characteristics:

- **Named attribution.** Notes are prefixed with the speaker where it matters. Achebe takes design,
  endpoints, recruitment, and activation; Ostrander takes everything operational.
- **The honest aside.** Every section should contain at least one sentence a polished sponsor deck
  would not contain: what sites actually do wrong, what the CRA will find, what the participant will
  actually say.
- **Detection, not exhortation.** Where the note warns against misconduct, it explains the mechanism
  by which the misconduct is discovered. This is both truer and more persuasive than moralising.
- **Forward-looking specificity.** Ostrander knows this drug from other sites. He can say "you will
  see fever" before the protocol formalises it, because that is precisely the kind of knowledge a CRA
  carries between sites and the kind that never appears in a manual.
- **Restraint.** Not every slide needs a revelation. Some notes are two lines. The document is more
  credible for the variation.
